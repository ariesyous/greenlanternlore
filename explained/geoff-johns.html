Geoff Johns and Green Lantern, Explained

Nine years. One writer. The run that turned a dying franchise into the backbone of the DC Universe.

In 2004, Green Lantern was a franchise on life support. Hal Jordan — the Silver Age icon — had spent a decade as a dead villain. The Corps was extinct. The book starred Kyle Rayner, a great character with a fraction of Hal's history, carrying the torch alone. Then DC handed the keys to Geoff Johns, a writer who'd grown up obsessed with the mythology, and told him to fix it.

What followed was one of the most consequential runs in modern comics.

The rebuild

Johns started with Green Lantern: Rebirth (2004), a six-issue miniseries that did the impossible: it brought Hal Jordan back and made his return make sense. The masterstroke was the Parallax retcon — revealing that Hal's madness in Emerald Twilight was the work of a fear entity imprisoned in the Battery. It didn't erase the dark chapter; it gave it a cause, and Hal a path to redemption. In one move, Johns honored the past decade of stories while restoring the hero.

But Rebirth was just the foundation. Over the next nine years, Johns systematically rebuilt the entire mythology from a single idea: emotion is light, and light can be wielded. The emotional spectrum — seven colors, seven corps, seven entities — turned Green Lantern from "space cops with rings" into a cosmic epic about the nature of feeling itself.

The saga

The run unfolds like a novel in chapters, each bigger than the last:

The Sinestro Corps War (2007) introduced the yellow corps and proved the concept could sustain an event. Rage of the Red Lanterns and Agent Orange (2008–09) added rage and avarice. Then came Blackest Night (2009–10) — the culmination, where all nine corps united against death itself. It was DC's biggest event in years, and it worked because Johns had spent five years laying every pipe.

The run closed with War of the Green Lanterns (2011), bringing the story full circle: the Corps, the entities, and the four Earth Lanterns who'd carried the saga.

Why it worked

Three things. First, Johns respects continuity — he doesn't reboot, he redeems. Every embarrassing old story becomes load-bearing. Second, he writes mythology, not just plots — the spectrum, the oaths, the prophecies all feel ancient and inevitable. Third, he never forgot it's about people — Hal's recklessness, John's discipline, Guy's bluster, Kyle's heart. The rings are cool. The Lanterns matter more.

Love it or hate it, the Johns run is the reason Green Lantern survived — and the reason this site exists. Start with Rebirth. You won't stop.
