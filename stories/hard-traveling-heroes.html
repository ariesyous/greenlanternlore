The classic route · 1970s

Hard-Traveling
Heroes

Hal Jordan and Oliver Queen take their argument about justice onto America's roads.

Follow the classics reading route

Choose this route for character and history

Prerequisites: none beyond the two leads. Hal Jordan is a Green Lantern used to enforcing cosmic law. Oliver Queen is Green Arrow, an outspoken human archer who challenges the distance between law and justice. Their disagreement drives a journey through problems that cannot simply be punched, contained in a construct, or handed to a space tribunal.

Dennis O'Neil and Neal Adams began this collaboration with Green Lantern #76 in 1970. Read it as a series of social and moral confrontations, not a modern event crossover. The art's human expressions and physical staging matter as much as the arguments. Racism, poverty, and drug abuse are central subjects; the stories also carry the language, assumptions, and dramatic conventions of their publication era. DC's account of #76 explains the run's starting point and road-trip premise.

A precise, manageable reading commitment

This guide follows the 2019 Hard-Traveling Heroes New Edition, a 300-page selection. Its contents are stories from:

  • Green Lantern/Green Arrow #76–87 and #89.
  • The Flash #217–219 and #226.

That is thirteen Green Lantern issue numbers plus selected material from four Flash issues, not seventeen complete issues of equal length. Read the collection in its printed order. The missing #88 is not a typo in this guide: the list follows DC's edition-specific contents. Other volumes and the much larger omnibus collect different material; matching a title alone is not enough.

O'Neil and Adams are the central creative pairing, with work by Elliot S. Maggin, Dick Giordano, and others in the selected collection. “Hard-Traveling Heroes” describes the celebrated body of stories; it is not the original title of a separate twelve-issue miniseries.

What to notice

Watch the gap between Hal's confidence in his authority and what people on Earth need from him. Oliver supplies an urgent challenge, but he is also a participant with blind spots. Reading the pair as people learning from encounters is more rewarding than treating either as an infallible spokesperson. Black Canary and the wider supporting cast keep those encounters from remaining a private debate between two men.

This is pre-Crisis material, published decades before the modern emotional-spectrum expansion. The later Parallax explanation and the nine classic Corps are not prerequisites, and applying every later ring rule retroactively can obscure what the original stories are doing. For the contemporary Lantern framework, return to the spectrum guide afterward.

Spoilers: two lasting developments in the selected issues

In #85, Oliver discovers Roy Harper's heroin addiction, forcing a public moral stance into a personal relationship. DC identifies that revelation in its #85 facsimile listing. #87 introduces John Stewart, initially selected as a substitute Lantern. His arrival gives the collection a second legacy beyond Hal and Oliver's partnership. DC's John Stewart profile confirms the debut.

Where to go next

Follow the new recruit into a much later solo spotlight with John Stewart's War Journal, or shift to the modern Hal saga with Green Lantern: Rebirth. For the earlier, magical branch of the legacy, meet Alan Scott. Those are different eras and useful comparisons, not consecutive chapters of this road trip.
